A simulated lab that can "do" chemistry experiments

Give it a paper or a procedure: the LLM reads the steps and writes a standard plan; the system sets up instruments on a real-size (1.8 m × 0.75 m) bench, runs in real time, reports a conclusion, and tallies what the human or robot arm did and how much labour the LLM saved.

Why the simulator can simulate chemistry

It does not play pre-recorded animations; it is a world advanced by physical-chemistry laws: with every drop added and every degree of heating, the solution's composition, temperature, pH and colour are recomputed, and instruments "measure" noisy readings from that true state. Four main layers:

① Mass balance and solution chemistry

Each container tracks the moles of every component. Adding, transferring, separating and filtering are booked by conserving volume and concentration; acid–base, complexation, precipitation and redox are solved together from equilibrium constants (component method), giving pH, species concentrations and precipitate amounts.

② Reaction kinetics and heat

Reactions advance by rate laws, with Arrhenius temperature dependence; reaction heat, hotplates, water baths and heat loss all enter the energy balance. So "heat 30 min" and "heat 60 min" give different yields, and calorimetry experiments can measure enthalpy changes.

③ Domain models

Polymers (radical polymerisation, step-growth molecular weight), interfaces (surface tension, contact angle, micelles), foams (foaming, half-life, coarsening), gold nanoparticles (nucleation and growth), enzymes and microbes (Michaelis–Menten, Monod growth), gases and high pressure, continuous-flow reactors, and more.

④ Imperfect instruments and operations

Each instrument is profiled from a real model (range, accuracy, response time, noise), so readings have error and lag; syringe pumps have syringe capacity and selector valves, tubing and probe cables have length limits, the robot arm can fail a grasp and retry, and manual operations take time.

Key point: ground truth and the "ledger" are separate. The simulator knows the true state internally, but the experimenter (LLM, human or robot arm) sees only instrument readings and its own operation log, and must infer results by measurement, just like a real experiment; scoring then compares the result with the truth to check it is within tolerance.

How it works

1 Read the paperFetch the full text, extract the experimental section (reagents, amounts, conditions, steps)
→
2 Write the planThe LLM turns the steps into a standard plan (humans can also fill in a table)
→
3 CheckThe checker verifies instruments, reagents and reactions, flags gaps, and dry-runs the whole plan in simulation
→
4 Tidy the benchPlace only the instruments needed, with tubing / probe-linked ones side by side; one-off assembly is done up front
→
5 ExecuteRun in real time (fast-forward allowed); the robot arm or a human operates step by step, with steps and instruments highlighted in sync
→
6 Conclude and scoreReport the measured result, error vs truth, human / robot division of work, and labour saved

Simulated and real devices share one interface: "send dispense to pump_1" in a plan drives the pump model in simulation, and with real hardware the same command goes to the real pump over serial. So a plan verified here can be moved to a real bench unchanged.

Limits and honest notes

  • Physical constants come from common textbook tables, mostly not checked line by line against original literature; instrument profile specs marked verified = false still need verification.
  • Domain models are "good-enough simplifications" (e.g. a phenomenological foam model): they reproduce trends and magnitudes but do not replace first-principles calculation.
  • When reproducing papers, instruments or reactions missing from the simulator are explicitly flagged as gaps, never silently skipped.
